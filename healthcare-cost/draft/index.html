NigeriaPrices at four elections and now

What does healthcare cost the average Nigerian now compared with each election?

In naira, healthcare costs far more than in 2011. Against prices in general, it has not clearly got worse.

Almost everything Nigerians buy now costs far more than in 2011. Healthcare did too, but the evidence we have does not show it rising faster than other prices. What has clearly changed is how many days of work the minimum wage needs for some basics.

Prices in general and official health prices · 2011 to 2026

Official health prices rose about 7×. Prices in general rose about 9×.

“Prices in general” is the Consumer Price Index (CPI), the official monthly measure from the National Bureau of Statistics. The blue line is its Health part. Prices in general rose about 10% a year until 2015, 14% a year to 2019, 17% to 2023 and 24% a year since. Official health prices followed the same path (8%, 10%, 15% and 22% a year). The pace picked up at every election, and the steepest rise has come since 2023.

What we can’t tell you yet

  • Where. Many sources do not say where the price was found; where they do, it is mostly Lagos and the South West.
  • Who sells. Early prices often come from insurance price lists and studies. Recent ones come from news reports and online shops. These are not the same kind of price.
  • Gaps. We have no 2011 price for an ultrasound. Antenatal care, normal delivery and caesarean section are held back: the prices we found are for different services in different places. A ₦77 booking fee in rural Kaduna sits beside a ₦50,000 whole-pregnancy package, and a rural clinic in 2012 beside a Lagos teaching hospital in 2019. They are in the table below, marked “no”. We will chart them once we have the same hospital or place at several dates.
  • Not a survey. We collected prices that were published. We did not sample hospitals at random.

What this adds up to

Prices rose a lot. Some rose far more than others.

  • Prices in general are about 9× their 2011 level; official health prices about 7×.
  • Across the 11 items we chart, the latest price is between 1.8× (blood-pressure medicine) and 9.4× (amoxicillin-clavulanate 625mg) the first price we found. The middle item is 4.0×.
  • The minimum wage is 3.9× higher but buys about 42% of what it did in 2011.

Where do these numbers come from?

Where did the prices come from?

From published studies, government and insurance price lists, hospital fees, news reports and online pharmacy listings. Every price links to its source in the table below. Each has a strength grade: A (survey or official tariff), B (single study or a reporter’s own visit), C (news quote, blog, forum, online listing).

How did you compare prices from different years?

Item by item, we take the middle (median) price we found in each election period and compare it with the first period’s. “Times the first price” is the later median divided by the first. Naira lost value over these years, which is why the headline tiles use the official price index (CPI) from the National Bureau of Statistics: prices in general are about 9× their 2011 level and official health prices about 7×. The charts show actual prices paid, not prices rewritten at today’s value.

How sure are you?

For the headline numbers: very, they are official statistics. For individual items: less, because few prices exist for each election. We put the 80 comparable prices (leaving out the “no” rows of the table) through one statistical model that allows for each item and each kind of source. Prices in naira in 2023–27 came out about 3.0× the 2011–15 level (range 2.0–4.6). After allowing for general price rises they came out about 0.5× (range 0.33–0.81): lower than 2011–15, not higher. We read this carefully and do not claim healthcare got cheaper: many early figures are insurance price lists, recent ones are news and online prices, and each period has few prices. What the evidence does not support is a claim that healthcare prices rose faster than prices in general.

What does “days of minimum-wage work” assume?

One day’s pay is the monthly minimum wage divided by 30 (₦18,000 until 2019, ₦30,000 from April 2019, ₦70,000 from July 2024). Dividing by about 22 working days instead would lower every figure by about a quarter without changing the pattern. We have not decided which to publish.

Words used on this page.

Prices in general (inflation)
How much the cost of everyday things rises over time.
CPI (Consumer Price Index)
The official measure of that rise. The National Bureau of Statistics publishes it each month.
Times (×) the first price
How many times bigger a price is than the first one we found. 2× means it doubled.
Minimum wage
The legal monthly minimum: ₦18,000 (2011), ₦30,000 (2019), ₦70,000 (2024).
Insurance price list (tariff)
The price a health insurance scheme pays a provider. Often lower than what patients pay.

Source and method. Consilium compilation of 143 published prices (80 charted, 63 held out for the reasons shown in the table); National Bureau of Statistics, Consumer Price Index, September 2026 (2024 = 100). Multiple = latest median price ÷ first median price; for example amoxicillin: ₦1,260 ÷ ₦315 = 4.0×. Minimum wage: ₦18,000 (2011), ₦30,000 (April 2019), ₦70,000 (July 2024).
